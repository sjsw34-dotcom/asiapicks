## Where to start if this is your first trip

Two decisions shape a Seoul trip more than any other: which neighbourhood you sleep in, and whether you are comfortable on the subway. Seoul is large and its attractions are scattered, so a well-placed base saves more time than any itinerary trick.

The city sits on both banks of the Han River. North of the river holds the five Joseon palaces, the old hanok quarters and the student and nightlife districts around Hongdae. South of the river is Gangnam, which is newer, wider and built around business and shopping rather than historic sights. Most first-time visitors spend the majority of their time north of the river.

## Do you need a visa or a K-ETA?

Nationals of visa-free countries and regions can enter South Korea without a K-ETA until 31 December 2026. The Ministry of Justice extended this temporary exemption to support the tourism industry, and the Korea Tourism Organization published the extension in December 2025. Travellers from countries that are not on the visa-free list still need a visa, and the exemption does not change that.

Applying for a K-ETA is still possible during the exemption and carries one practical benefit: K-ETA holders are not asked to fill in the paper arrival card. Check the official K-ETA site for the current country list before booking, because the exemption has an explicit end date and has been extended year by year rather than made permanent.

## How much does getting around cost?

A Seoul subway ride starts at 1,550 won with a transit card, or 1,650 won paying cash for a single-journey ticket. The base fare rose from 1,400 won on 28 June 2025 and applies across Seoul, Incheon and Gyeonggi Province, so the same fare structure covers most of the capital region. Longer journeys add to the base fare by distance.

A transit card is worth buying on arrival rather than paying cash each trip. The card is cheaper per ride, and transfers between subway lines and buses are handled automatically when you tap out and tap in again. ## When to go

Autumn, from late September through early November, is the most reliable stretch of weather: clear skies, mild temperatures and the foliage season in the mountains ringing the city. Spring, late March into May, brings cherry blossom and similar temperatures, and draws comparable crowds.

Summer is hot and humid, and the East Asian monsoon delivers the year's heaviest rain in late June and July. Winter is cold and dry, with the compensation of low-season accommodation prices and the shortest queues at the palaces. There is no wrong season for Seoul, because the subway and the covered markets make bad weather manageable, but late September through early November is the season we would choose first.
