## What is Bukchon Hanok Village?

Bukchon is a residential neighbourhood of hanok, Korea's tile-roofed wooden houses, on the slope between Gyeongbokgung and Changdeokgung palaces. People live in the houses you walk past. Visit Seoul describes it as a village of Seoulites that was not built for tourists, unlike Namsangol Hanok Village, which is.

The name means "northern village", for its place north of Cheonggyecheon Stream and Jongno, according to the Korea Tourism Organization. The houses date from the Joseon dynasty, and some now hold galleries, workshops, guesthouses, tea houses and restaurants among the homes.

There is no ticket, no entrance and no closing gate. What limits a visit is a set of district rules written to protect the residents, and they decide when the best-known lanes can be seen at all.

## When can tourists enter the Bukchon red zone?

From 10am to 5pm. Jongno-gu designated Bukchon a special management zone on 1 July 2024 and made about 34,000 square metres around Bukchon-ro 11-gil a red zone, closed to tourists from 5pm to 10am the next morning. After a guidance period, fines of {{bukchon-red-zone-fine}} have applied since 1 March 2025.

The district's definition of tourism is wide. Photographing or filming, stopping to look around, and wandering the lanes for sightseeing all count. Residents, their family and friends, merchants, shop customers and people passing through are exempt, but only while they are not doing any of those things, so a guest staying in the zone who stops to film the rooftops at 7pm is back under the rule.

Two lighter zones sit around it. The yellow zone, north of the red zone along Bukchon-ro 12-gil, is monitored without hours or fines. Seoul Metropolitan Government's notice also lists an orange zone under intensive guidance, again with no hours limit. In practice, only the red zone changes when you can walk.

## Can you visit Bukchon on a coach tour?

Not by coach into the lanes. Since 1 January 2026, chartered buses are banned at all hours, weekends and holidays included, on about 2.3km of Bukchon's streets, among them Bukchon-ro, Bukchon-ro 5-gil and Changdeokgung 1-gil. Operators are fined 300,000 won for a first offence, 400,000 won for a second and 500,000 won for a third.

Commuter buses, school buses and the local village buses are exempt. For a visitor, the effect is that group tours now set down outside the district and walk in, which is worth knowing when a tour description promises Bukchon.

## How do you get to Bukchon Hanok Village?

Take Line 3 to Anguk Station. Seoul Metropolitan Government's directions leave from exit 2 and follow Bukchon-ro straight uphill for about 600m to the Gahoe-dong lanes. The Korea Tourism Organization starts its walk from exit 3, beside the Bukchon Traditional Culture Center, which it calls the base camp for a Bukchon visit.

Anguk sits between Gyeongbokgung Station and Jongno 3-ga Station on Line 3, so either palace station is a single stop away. The Korea Tourism Organization notes there is no parking for visitors, so the subway is the way in.

## What is a good walking route through Bukchon?

Follow the Bukchon 8 Views, a numbered set of viewpoints the Korea Tourism Organization lays out in order from the Anguk end:

1. Changdeokgung seen from above, on the eastern edge.
2. The craft workshop street in Wonseo-dong.
3. The lanes of Gahoe-dong 11.
4. The hill at Gahoe-dong 31, with the Bukchon viewpoint.
5. The uphill Gahoe-dong alley.
6. The downhill Gahoe-dong alley.
7. Gahoe-dong 31 itself.
8. The stone steps down to Samcheong-dong.

The middle of that route, the Gahoe-dong lanes that Seoul Metropolitan Government singles out as among the best-preserved hanok paths in the city, is where Bukchon-ro 11-gil runs. That is the red zone, so the whole walk needs to fit inside 10am to 5pm, and ideally well before the 5pm cut-off.

Our pick is to start the numbered views at about 10:30am, reach Gahoe-dong before midday, and let the stone steps take you down into Samcheong-dong for lunch. The Korea Tourism Organization's course then continues through Samcheong-dong and Gamgodang-gil and ends in Insadong, which is the easy way out of the district without doubling back.

## How does Bukchon fit with Gyeongbokgung and Changdeokgung?

Put one palace on each side of the walk. Gyeongbokgung is to the west and Changdeokgung to the east, both open from 9am to 6pm in September and October with last entry at 5pm. Adult tickets are {{seoul-palace-admission-adult}} each, and the palace authority admits visitors wearing hanbok free.

Watch the closing days. Gyeongbokgung is closed on Tuesdays and Changdeokgung on Mondays, so a day with both palaces and Bukchon works from Wednesday to Sunday. When a closing day falls on a public holiday, the palace opens and closes on the next working day instead.

A morning at Gyeongbokgung from 9am puts you in Bukchon just after the red zone opens, and the walk can finish on the Changdeokgung side for the afternoon. The full day, with the Secret Garden tour and a market dinner, is laid out in the Seoul itinerary.

## What etiquette does Bukchon ask of visitors?

Treat it as someone's street, because it is. Visit Seoul's "silent tourism" guidance asks visitors to keep their voices low and not to litter, to walk in groups of no more than 10, and not to use microphones or loudspeakers.

Photos need the most care. Visit Seoul asks visitors not to photograph the inside of houses, even when a door stands open, and a hanok gate is a front door, not a set. Take pictures from the lane, not from inside a gateway.

The rest follows from the same idea. Bukchon's houses are private unless a sign says a shop, gallery or tea house is open, and a closed door is closed.

## Where should you stay to walk Bukchon early?

Near Anguk or Insadong. A base within walking distance lets you reach the numbered views soon after 10am without planning a subway ride around the red zone's opening time. The trade-offs between Jongno and the other Seoul areas are compared in the where-to-stay guide.
