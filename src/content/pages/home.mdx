Planning a first trip to South Korea comes down to four decisions: how long to go for, which month, whether you need paperwork before you fly, and how you will move around once you land. This page answers each one with a figure and a source, then points you at the guide that goes deeper.

## How many days do you need?

Five days covers Seoul properly: the palace district, one market, one modern neighbourhood and a half-day trip out of the city. Eight to ten days adds Busan or Jeju without rushing either. Two weeks lets you add Gyeongju and slow down.

The reason short trips still work is the KTX. Seoul to Busan takes about 2 hours 15 minutes on the fastest services, which is why a second city is realistic even on a week-long trip. Distance is rarely the constraint in Korea. Deciding what to skip is.

## Which month should you pick?

October is the most reliable month, and the gap is wide enough to plan around. Seoul averages a low of 9.9°C and a high of 20.1°C in October, with roughly 50mm of rain across the entire month. July averages 415mm. Those are 1991 to 2020 climate normals, so they describe the pattern rather than a promise about any single year.

April is the other mild window at 7.4°C to 17.8°C, with cherry blossom and crowds to match. Summer is hot, humid and wet, with the changma rains concentrated from late June through July. Winter is cold and dry, and buys you low-season room rates and the shortest queues at the palaces.

## Do you need a visa or a K-ETA?

Nationals of visa-free countries and regions can enter South Korea without a K-ETA until 31 December 2026. The Ministry of Justice extended this temporary exemption to support tourism, and the Korea Tourism Organization published the extension in December 2025. Travellers from countries that are not on the visa-free list still need a visa, and the exemption does not change that.

Two things are worth knowing before you rely on it. The exemption has an explicit end date and has been extended year by year rather than made permanent, so check the official K-ETA site for the current country list close to your trip. And applying anyway carries one small benefit: K-ETA holders are not asked to fill in the paper arrival card.

## What does getting around cost?

A Seoul subway ride starts at 1,550 won with a transit card, or 1,650 won in cash. The fare rose from 1,400 won on 28 June 2025 and covers Seoul, Incheon and Gyeonggi Province.

Busan runs a separate network with its own fares: 1,600 won for one section and 1,800 won for two on a transit card, according to Busan Transportation Corporation. The systems are separate but the card is not, so one transit card covers both cities and the buses in each.

A caution worth carrying into any Korea research you do. Seoul's official English tourism site still shows the old 1,400 won fare, and plenty of widely-read guides repeat it. A page quoting 1,400 won is stale rather than describing a cheaper ticket. It is a useful test: if a Korea guide still lists 1,400 won, check the rest of its numbers too.
